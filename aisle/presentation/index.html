Be There

Navigation for blind and low-vision people that doesn't stop at the front door.

No beacons.

No store partnership.

No extra hardware.

One phone.

Aisle Be There is not a chatbot and not a smart-glasses demo. It is one iOS app that a blind person can run today on the phone they already own. Everything in this deck is built, running, and field-tested in a real grocery store. Four of us, one weekend.

The gap

Maps gets you to the address. Then it goes quiet.

Aisle Be There covers the last hundred metres and the last arm's length.

The last mile is solved. The last hundred metres and the last arm's length are not. Inside a store there is no map at all, describer apps narrate instead of guiding — "a refrigerator section with several doors" does not tell you which way to walk — and asking a stranger costs the independence that was the point of the errand. That is the whole gap we went after.

One errand, three handoffs

Each handoff is automatic. The user never picks a mode.

Most assistive tools do exactly one of these three. The hard engineering is the seam between them — knowing you have entered the store, knowing you have reached the right shelf, knowing to switch from walking cues to hand cues. Under it sit three tiers: on-device ARKit, CoreML and Vision OCR every frame for anything a haptic depends on; Claude on occasional still frames for the hard visual questions; and Nemotron as the decision layer. Street and store video never leaves the phone.

The design rule everything else follows from

Silence is the reward.

On course, it says nothing. Twelve words maximum when it does. Six words are banned outright, and it never decides a crossing.

This is what separates us from a camera wired to an LLM. The banned list — safe, clear, go, cross now, no cars, you can cross — is a real linter in the repo: a build that would speak any of them fails. The twelve-word cap is checked at build time too. That constraint changed the architecture, because if you cannot say "go" you have to be genuinely useful with position and haptics instead. Four haptic patterns carry most of it: COURSE, TURN, STOP, CONFIRM.

Out Loud

Our user cannot look at the screen. Ever.

A text box would not make this project worse. It would make it useless.

Every output is audio or haptic. Every input can be a held button and a sentence. The screen is a courtesy for the sighted person helping with setup.

This is the one sentence the track asks for, and for us it is not a stretch: the user is blind, walking, and has one hand on a cane or a dog. Voice is not the interface we chose, it is the only channel available. That is also why we are strict about it — an interface you cannot look away from has to earn every word it spends.

Out Loud  ·  the voice stack

508 phrases, synthesized once and shipped in the binary.

ElevenLabs Flash v2.5 speaks every line, pre-rendered at build time. On the street the phrase is already on the phone, so a cue costs no network and cannot be delayed by a dead bar of signal.

Three backends behind one priority queue: cached clip → live /api/tts → expo-speech offline. Input is on-device recognition with Scribe v2 as the fallback.

The point of this slide is that the voice work is infrastructure, not a wrapper. Every phrase in the closed table is synthesized ahead of time and normalized to one loudness, so cues do not jump in volume between a cached clip and a live one. The histogram is the twelve-word rule showing up as physics: nearly everything lands under three seconds, because a cue that runs longer than that is a cue the walker has already outrun. A recording is deleted the moment the utterance ends — no voice audio ever accumulates on the phone.

Beyond the Chatbot

Nemotron never talks to the user.

It is the decision layer behind the app: JSON in, JSON out, executed by code rather than read aloud. Six jobs.

parseIntent routeCompile taskPlan disambiguate crossingAnnounce answer

nvidia/nemotron-3.5-lightning-30b-a3b  ·  it never sees an image

The track slide. Nemotron is not the thing the user talks to — its output is parsed, schema-validated and executed. If it returned prose the app would break, not degrade. It races a deterministic local template and the first valid answer wins, so a timeout is a slightly plainer cue, never an outage. Nothing time-critical waits on it. It also never touches a pixel: Claude is the one that sees images.

Read the honest shape of this chart out loud rather than hiding it. Nemotron is slower than Haiku on five of the six jobs, and on routeCompile it is four and a half times slower with two timeouts. It wins on answer. We kept it first on routeCompile anyway because the directional wording is the thing we actually want from it, and now the cost of that choice is a number instead of an assumption. This is also the argument for the race existing at all: parseIntent already flipped its primary to Haiku automatically after five samples with a rolling median over three seconds, so the routing rule is live, not decorative.

Evidence

We measured it, and we broke it.

template (fallback) planner race (live)

The deterministic template still wins overall, 98.3 % to 93.3 %. That is why the race exists rather than a bare model call.

Two failures found in the NIM endpoint: guided_json returns HTTP 400, and streaming's first chunk takes ~34 s where the whole non-streamed answer takes ~1 s. Both are dated in our source.

METHOD Set 60 held utterances — 20 clean, 20 carrying real ASR corruption, 20 deliberately off-task — scored against labelled intents over a 9-way classification (find_item, navigate_to, guided_task, repeat, how_far, where_am_i, abort, help, unknown). Arms template is the deterministic on-device classifier alone; race is the live Nemotron/Haiku race with the template as fallback, scored end to end including that fallback (which fired on 1.7 %). Also measured item match on find_item 100 % template vs 95 % race; task-plan grounding 5/5 for both, where step one must name an observed landmark and keep its observed side. Failures, named template misses one — "how fart is it" → unknown. The race misses four — "take me to the milk" → navigate_to, "instructions" → repeat, "open the door" and "turn on the lights" → guided_task. Full confusion matrices in server/routes/plan.eval.md. Separately 10,313 trace records from one failed grocery run, read line by line, produced the two fixes shipped since.

Be honest about the headline: the deterministic template beat the live planner race on raw intent accuracy, 98.3 to 93.3 — which is exactly why the race exists. What Nemotron buys is the grounded multi-step task plan and the phrasings the template has no rule for. The NIM findings are the track's "failure you found": we measured them, dated them in a comment, and shipped non-streaming json_object mode with the schema in the prompt instead of discovering it in a store. The 10,313 records came from a grocery run that went wrong — context defaulting to home, and relative depth used as a hard veto on movement cues. Say plainly which of those are fixed in the build you just demoed.

Seed Round

The infrastructure answer was tried. It does not arrive.

~5%

of New York's signalized intersections have accessible pedestrian signals — under 2 % in Chicago. Waiting for the city is not a plan.

60%

of crossings end outside the crosswalk without a far-side cue; 26 % with one. The average traveller drifts ~5 m over 22 m.

100%

retrieval when blind shoppers follow template aisle directions — ShopTalk, Utah State. The method works. Nobody shipped it to a phone.

SOURCES & STATUS Figures accessible-pedestrian-signal coverage and the crossing-drift study are the published figures cited in 00-PROJECT-BRIEF.md; ShopTalk is Utah State's template-direction trial, verbal and camera-free. The counter-example we respect the only peer-reviewed test of a general VLM judging crossing risk for blind users scored 25 % and confused approaching with distant vehicles — which is why no cloud model decides a crossing in this app. Why now no beacons, no store partnership, no extra hardware, so every store is covered on day one instead of after a procurement cycle. GoodMaps needs a LiDAR survey; Waymap and Lazarillo need surveying or beacon installs. Working product, not a deck 1,029 Jest tests across 70 suites and 183 vitest across 19 files green on this checkout, typecheck and phrase lint clean in CI. Not yet true nobody has been charged, and it has not been tested with blind users — sighted people with their eyes shut is not the same evidence.

The Seed Round slide. The problem is not that nobody tried; it is that every existing answer needs somebody else to install something first — signals the city has not funded, beacons the store has not bought, glasses the user has not got. Ours needs a phone they already own, which is the only distribution story that starts at zero. Who has the problem: blind and low-vision travellers doing ordinary errands, every day, not the lab-visit version. If asked about money, be straight — we have not charged anyone, the honest next step is testing with blind users rather than sighted people with their eyes shut.
